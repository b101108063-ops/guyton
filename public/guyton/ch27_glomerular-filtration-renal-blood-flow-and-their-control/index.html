Glomerular Filtration, Renal Blood Flow, and Their Control

MYOGENIC AUTOREGULATION OF RENAL BLOOD FLOW AND GLOMERULAR FILTRATION RATE

Another mechanism contributes to the maintenance of a relatively constant renal blood flow and GFR: the ability of individual blood vessels to resist stretching during increased arterial pressure.

This is referred to as the myogenic mechanism.

Studies of individual blood vessels (especially small arterioles) throughout the body have shown that they respond to increased wall tension or wall stretch by contraction of the vascular smooth muscle.

Stretch of the vascular wall allows increased movement of calcium ions from the extracellular fluid into the cells, causing them to contract through the mechanisms discussed in Chapter 8.

This contraction prevents excessive stretch of the vessel. By raising vascular resistance, it also helps prevent excessive increases in renal blood flow and GFR when arterial pressure increases.

Although the myogenic mechanism probably operates in most arterioles throughout the body, its importance in renal blood flow and GFR autoregulation has been questioned because this pressure-sensitive mechanism has no means of directly detecting changes in renal blood flow or GFR per se.

On the other hand, this mechanism may be more important in protecting the kidney from hypertension-induced injury.

In response to sudden increases in blood pressure, the myogenic constrictor response in afferent arterioles occurs within seconds. It attenuates transmission of increased arterial pressure to the glomerular capillaries.

TUBULOGLOMERULAR FEEDBACK

Tubuloglomerular feedback (macula densa mechanism) links distal tubular sodium chloride delivery to afferent arteriolar resistance. This helps maintain constant GFR and renal blood flow despite changes in arterial pressure.

HIGH PROTEIN INTAKE AND HYPERGLYCEMIA INCREASE RENAL BLOOD FLOW AND GLOMERULAR FILTRATION RATE

Although renal blood flow and GFR are relatively stable under most conditions, there are circumstances in which these variables change significantly.

A high protein intake is known to increase renal blood flow and GFR. With a long-term, high-protein diet, such as one containing large amounts of meat, increases in GFR and renal blood flow are due partly to growth of the kidneys.

However, the GFR and renal blood flow also increase 20% to 30% within 1 or 2 hours after a person eats a high-protein meal.

Mechanism of Increased GFR After High Protein Intake

One likely explanation for the increased GFR is the following:

  1. A high-protein meal releases into the blood amino acids
  2. Amino acids are reabsorbed in the proximal tubules
  3. Because amino acids and sodium are reabsorbed together by cotransport in the proximal tubules, increased amino acid reabsorption also stimulates sodium reabsorption
  4. This increased reabsorption of sodium decreases sodium delivery to the macula densa
  5. This elicits a tubuloglomerular feedback–mediated decrease in resistance of the afferent arterioles
  6. The decreased afferent arteriolar resistance then raises renal blood flow and GFR
  7. This allows sodium excretion to be maintained at a nearly normal level while increasing excretion of the waste products of protein metabolism, such as urea

Similar Mechanism in Diabetes

A similar mechanism may explain the marked increases in renal blood flow and GFR that occur with large increases in blood glucose levels in persons with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.

Glucose, like some amino acids, is also reabsorbed along with sodium in the proximal tubule. Increased glucose delivery to the tubules causes them to reabsorb excess sodium along with glucose.

This increased reabsorption of sodium decreases the sodium chloride concentration at the macula densa. This activates tubuloglomerular feedback–mediated dilation of the afferent arterioles, and subsequent increases in renal blood flow and GFR occur.

Purpose of Tubuloglomerular Feedback

These examples demonstrate that renal blood flow and GFR per se are not the primary variables controlled by the tubuloglomerular feedback mechanism.

The main purpose of this feedback is to ensure a constant delivery of sodium chloride to the distal tubule, where final processing of the urine takes place.

Disturbances that tend to increase the reabsorption of sodium chloride at tubular sites before the macula densa tend to elicit increased renal blood flow and GFR. This helps return distal sodium chloride delivery toward normal, allowing normal rates of sodium and water excretion to be maintained.

Opposite Sequence With Reduced Proximal Reabsorption

An opposite sequence of events occurs when proximal tubular reabsorption is reduced. For example, when the proximal tubules are damaged (by poisoning from heavy metals such as mercury, or large doses of drugs such as tetracyclines), their ability to reabsorb sodium chloride is decreased.

Large amounts of sodium chloride are then delivered to the distal tubule. Without appropriate compensation, this would quickly cause excessive volume depletion.

One important compensatory response appears to be tubuloglomerular feedback–mediated renal vasoconstriction, which occurs in response to the increased sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa.

These examples again demonstrate the importance of this feedback mechanism, ensuring that the distal tubules receive the proper rate of delivery of:

  • Sodium chloride
  • Other tubular fluid solutes
  • Tubular fluid volume

This allows appropriate amounts of these substances to be excreted in the urine.

OTHER FACTORS THAT INFLUENCE RENAL BLOOD FLOW AND GLOMERULAR FILTRATION RATE

The GFR and renal blood flow are low at birth. They approach normal adult levels after about 2 years of life.

In the absence of kidney disease, they are maintained relatively constant until the fourth decade. Thereafter, GFR declines by about 5% to 10% per decade, with considerable variability among individuals.

This decline in GFR coincides with:

  • Nitric oxide deficiency
  • Increased oxidative stress
  • Loss of functional nephrons

This may be related partly to:

  • Increasing blood pressure
  • Metabolic disorders
  • Other insults that cause cumulative glomerular injury with aging

Sex Differences

Men have higher renal blood flow and GFR than women, even when corrected for body mass. However, men also have a more rapid decline in GFR with aging than premenopausal women.

The mechanisms responsible for these sex differences are not fully understood. Beneficial effects of estrogens and damaging effects of androgens on the kidneys have been suggested as a partial explanation.

RENAL CLEARANCE

As the glomerular filtrate enters the renal tubules, it flows sequentially through successive parts:

  • Proximal tubule
  • Loop of Henle
  • Distal tubule
  • Collecting tubule
  • Collecting duct

It is then excreted as urine. Along this course:

  • Some substances are selectively reabsorbed from the tubules back into the blood
  • Other substances are secreted from the blood into the tubular lumen

The urine that is formed represents the sum of three basic renal processes:

  • Glomerular filtration
  • Tubular reabsorption
  • Tubular secretion

For many substances, tubular reabsorption plays a much more important role than secretion in determining the final urinary excretion rate. However, tubular secretion accounts for significant amounts of:

  • Potassium ions
  • Hydrogen ions
  • A few other substances

Key Observations From Filtration and Reabsorption Data

First observation: The processes of glomerular filtration and tubular reabsorption are quantitatively large relative to urinary excretion for many substances.

A small change in glomerular filtration or tubular reabsorption can potentially cause a relatively large change in urinary excretion. For example, a 10% decrease in tubular reabsorption (from 178.5 to 160.7 L/day) would increase urine volume from 1.5 to 19.3 L/day, almost a 13-fold increase if the GFR remained constant.

In reality, changes in tubular reabsorption and glomerular filtration are closely coordinated, and large fluctuations in urinary excretion are avoided.

Second observation: Unlike glomerular filtration, which is relatively nonselective, tubular reabsorption is highly selective.

Some substances, such as glucose and amino acids, are almost completely reabsorbed, and urinary excretion rate is essentially zero. Many ions in the plasma, such as sodium, chloride, and bicarbonate, are also highly reabsorbed, but their rates of reabsorption and urinary excretion are variable, depending on the needs of the body.

Waste products, such as urea and creatinine, conversely, are poorly reabsorbed and are excreted in relatively large amounts. By controlling their reabsorption of different substances, the kidneys regulate excretion of solutes independently of one another. This capability is essential for precise control of body fluid composition.
