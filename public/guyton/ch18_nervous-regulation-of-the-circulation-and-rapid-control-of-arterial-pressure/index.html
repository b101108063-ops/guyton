Nervous Regulation of the Circulation and Rapid Control of Arterial Pressure

AUTONOMIC NERVOUS SYSTEM

The most important part of the autonomic nervous system for regulating the circulation is the sympathetic nervous system. The parasympathetic nervous system contributes importantly to regulation of heart function.

Sympathetic Nervous System

Figure 18-1 shows the anatomy of sympathetic nervous control of the circulation. Sympathetic vasomotor nerve fibers leave the spinal cord through all the thoracic spinal nerves and through the first one or two lumbar spinal nerves.

These fibers then pass immediately into a sympathetic chain — one on each side of the vertebral column. From there, they pass by two routes to the circulation:

  1. Through specific sympathetic nerves that innervate mainly the vasculature of the internal viscera and the heart
  2. Almost immediately into peripheral portions of the spinal nerves distributed to the vasculature of the peripheral areas

Sympathetic Innervation of the Blood Vessels

Figure 18-2 shows the distribution of sympathetic nerve fibers to the blood vessels. In most tissues, all vessels except the capillaries are innervated.

  • Precapillary sphincters and metarterioles are innervated in some tissues (such as mesenteric blood vessels), although their sympathetic innervation is usually not as dense as in small arteries, arterioles, and veins.
  • Innervation of small arteries and arterioles allows sympathetic stimulation to increase resistance to blood flow and decrease the rate of blood flow through the tissues.
  • Innervation of large vessels — particularly veins — makes it possible for sympathetic stimulation to decrease the volume of these vessels. This can push blood into the heart and plays a major role in regulation of heart pumping.

Sympathetic Stimulation Increases Heart Rate and Contractility

Sympathetic fibers go directly to the heart (shown in Figure 18-1). As discussed in Chapter 9, sympathetic stimulation markedly increases the activity of the heart — both increasing the heart rate and enhancing its strength and volume of pumping.

Parasympathetic Stimulation Decreases Heart Rate and Contractility

Although the parasympathetic nervous system is exceedingly important for many other autonomic functions (such as control of multiple gastrointestinal actions), it plays only a minor role in regulating vascular function in most tissues.

Its most important circulatory effect is to control heart rate by way of parasympathetic nerve fibers to the heart in the vagus nerves (shown in Figure 18-1 by the dashed red line from the brain medulla directly to the heart).

Parasympathetic stimulation causes a marked decrease in heart rate and a slight decrease in heart muscle contractility.

SYMPATHETIC VASOCONSTRICTOR SYSTEM AND ITS CONTROL BY THE CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM

The sympathetic nerves carry large numbers of vasoconstrictor nerve fibers and only a few vasodilator fibers. The vasoconstrictor fibers are distributed to essentially all segments of the circulation, but more to some tissues than to others.

This sympathetic vasoconstrictor effect is especially powerful in the kidneys, intestines, spleen, and skin — but is much less potent in skeletal muscle, heart, and brain.

VASOMOTOR CENTER IN THE BRAIN

Located bilaterally mainly in the reticular substance of the medulla and lower third of the pons is an area called the vasomotor center (shown in Figures 18-1 and 18-3).

This center transmits:

  • Parasympathetic impulses through the vagus nerves to the heart
  • Sympathetic impulses through the spinal cord and peripheral sympathetic nerves to virtually all arteries, arterioles, and veins of the body

Areas of the Vasomotor Center

Although the total organization of the vasomotor center is still unclear, experiments have made it possible to identify certain important areas:

Area Location Function
Vasoconstrictor area Bilaterally in the anterolateral portions of the upper medulla Distributes fibers that cause vasoconstriction
Other areas (Not specified) Various regulatory functions

Sympathetic Vasoconstriction Effects

Sympathetic vasoconstriction affects different vascular beds differently:

Tissue Vasoconstrictor Effect
Kidneys Very powerful
Intestines Very powerful
Spleen Very powerful
Skin Very powerful
Skeletal muscle Much weaker
Heart Much weaker
Brain Much weaker
