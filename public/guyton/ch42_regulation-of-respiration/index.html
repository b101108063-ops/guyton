Regulation of Respiration

RESPIRATORY ABNORMALITIES

The previous chapters discussed several methods for studying respiratory abnormalities.

These methods include:

  • Vital capacity
  • Tidal air
  • Functional residual capacity
  • Dead space
  • Physiologic shunt
  • Physiological dead space

This array of measurements is only part of the armamentarium of the clinical pulmonary physiologist.

STUDY OF BLOOD GASES AND BLOOD pH

Among the most fundamental of all tests of pulmonary performance are determinations of:

  • Blood partial pressure of oxygen (PO2)
  • Blood partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2)
  • Blood pH

It is often important to make these measurements rapidly as an aid in determining appropriate therapy for:

  • Acute respiratory distress
  • Acute abnormalities of acid-base balance

The following simple and rapid methods have been developed to make these measurements within minutes, using no more than a few drops of blood.

DETERMINATION OF BLOOD pH

Blood pH is measured using a glass pH electrode of the type commonly used in chemical laboratories.

The electrodes used for this purpose are miniaturized.

The voltage generated by the glass electrode is a direct measure of pH.

This voltage is generally read directly from a voltmeter scale or recorded on a chart.

DETERMINATION OF BLOOD PCO2

A glass electrode pH meter can also be used to determine blood CO2.

When a weak solution of sodium bicarbonate is exposed to CO2 gas, the CO2 dissolves in the solution until an equilibrium state is reached.

DETERMINATION OF BLOOD PO2

The concentration of O2 in a fluid can be measured by a technique called polarography.

Procedure

  1. Electric current is made to flow between a small negative electrode and the solution
  2. If the voltage of the electrode is more than −0.6 volt different from the voltage of the solution, O2 will deposit on the electrode
  3. The rate of current flow through the electrode is directly proportional to the concentration of O2 (and therefore to PO2 as well)

Practical Implementation

In practice:

  • A negative platinum electrode with a surface area of about 1 square millimeter is used
  • This electrode is separated from the blood by a thin plastic membrane
  • The membrane allows diffusion of O2 but not diffusion of proteins or other substances that will “poison” the electrode

COMBINED MEASUREMENT DEVICE

Often, all three measuring devices for pH, PCO2, and PO2 are built into the same apparatus.

All these measurements can be made within a minute or so using a single droplet-sized sample of blood.

Thus, changes in blood gas levels and pH can be followed almost moment by moment at the bedside.

SUMMARY: CHEMICAL CONTROL OF RESPIRATION

The respiratory system maintains blood O2, CO2, and pH within narrow limits.

The following mechanisms are involved:

  • Central chemoreceptors in the medulla (respond to increased PCO2 and H+ in CSF)
  • Peripheral chemoreceptors in carotid and aortic bodies (respond mainly to low PO2)
  • Integration by the brainstem respiratory center
  • Feedback adjustments to alveolar ventilation

SUMMARY: HIGHER CORTICAL AND VOLUNTARY CONTROL

The cerebral cortex allows voluntary control of breathing for activities like speech, singing, and breath-holding.

The hypothalamus and limbic system can influence respiration during emotional states.

These override the automatic brainstem control but cannot permanently suppress it.
