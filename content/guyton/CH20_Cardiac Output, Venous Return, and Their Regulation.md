## CARDIAC OUTPUT AND VENOUS RETURN

**Cardiac output** is the quantity of blood pumped into the aorta each minute by the heart. This is also the quantity of blood that flows through the circulation. Because cardiac output is the sum of blood flow to all tissues of the body, it is one of the most important factors in cardiovascular function.

**Venous return** is the quantity of blood flowing from the veins into the right atrium each minute. Venous return and cardiac output must equal each other, except for a few heartbeats when blood is temporarily stored in or removed from the heart and lungs.

## NORMAL VALUES FOR CARDIAC OUTPUT AT REST AND DURING ACTIVITY

Cardiac output varies widely with the level of body activity. The following factors directly affect cardiac output:

1. The basic level of body metabolism
2. Whether the person is exercising
3. The person's age
4. The size of the body

### Normal Cardiac Output Values

For young healthy men, resting cardiac output averages about **5.6 L/min**. For women, this value is about **4.9 L/min**.

With increasing age, body activity and mass of some tissues (e.g., skeletal muscle) diminish. Therefore, the average cardiac output for the resting adult is often stated to be about **5 L/min**.

However, cardiac output varies considerably among healthy individuals depending on muscle mass, adiposity, physical activity, and other factors that influence metabolic rate and nutritional needs of the tissues.

### Cardiac Index

Cardiac output increases approximately in proportion to the **surface area** of the body. Therefore, cardiac output is frequently stated in terms of the **cardiac index** — cardiac output per square meter of body surface area.

The average person who weighs 70 kilograms has a body surface area of about **1.7 square meters**. Therefore, the normal average cardiac index for adults is about **3 L/min/m²**.

### Effect of Age on Cardiac Output

Figure 20-1 shows the cardiac index at different ages:

| Age | Cardiac Index |
|-----|---------------|
| 10 years | >4 L/min/m² |
| 80 years | ~2.4 L/min/m² |

The cardiac index rises rapidly to a level greater than 4 L/min/m² at age 10 years, then declines to about 2.4 L/min/m² at age 80 years.

The cardiac output is regulated throughout life almost directly in proportion to overall metabolic activity. Therefore, the declining cardiac index is indicative of declining activity and/or declining muscle mass with age.

## CONTROL OF CARDIAC OUTPUT BY VENOUS RETURN — FRANK-STARLING MECHANISM OF THE HEART

Although heart function is obviously crucial in determining cardiac output, the various factors of the peripheral circulation that affect flow of blood into the heart from the veins — called **venous return** — are normally the primary controllers of cardiac output.

### Frank-Starling Law of the Heart

The heart has a built-in mechanism that normally allows it to pump automatically the amount of blood that flows from the veins into the right atrium. This mechanism — called the **Frank-Starling law of the heart** (discussed in Chapter 9) — states:

When increased quantities of blood flow into the heart, the increased volume of blood stretches the walls of the heart chambers. As a result of the stretch, the cardiac muscle contracts with increased force. This action ejects the extra blood that has entered from the systemic circulation.

Therefore, the blood that flows into the heart is automatically pumped without delay into the aorta and flows again through the circulation.

### Bainbridge Reflex and Heart Rate

Stretching the heart also causes an increased heart rate (discussed in Chapters 10 and 18):

- **Stretch of the sinus node** in the wall of the right atrium has a direct effect on the rhythmicity of the node to increase the heart rate as much as 10% to 15%.
- The stretched right atrium initiates a nervous reflex called the **Bainbridge reflex** — passing first to the vasomotor center of the brain, then back to the heart by way of the sympathetic nerves and vagi — which also increases the heart rate.

Under most normal unstressed conditions, cardiac output is controlled mainly by peripheral factors that determine venous return. However, if the returning blood becomes more than the heart can pump, then the heart becomes the limiting factor.

## CARDIAC OUTPUT IS THE SUM OF ALL TISSUE BLOOD FLOWS

The venous return to the heart is the **sum of all the local blood flow** through all the individual tissue segments of the peripheral circulation (Figure 20-2). Therefore, cardiac output regulation is normally the sum of all the local blood flow regulations.

### Local Blood Flow Regulation

The mechanisms of local blood flow regulation were discussed in Chapter 17. In most tissues, blood flow increases mainly in proportion to each tissue's metabolism.

For example, local blood flow almost always increases when tissue oxygen consumption increases. Figure 20-3 demonstrates this for different levels of exercise — at each increasing level of work output during exercise, oxygen consumption and cardiac output increase in parallel to each other.

### Cardiac Output Varies Inversely With Total Peripheral Resistance

When arterial pressure is unchanged, the long-term cardiac output level varies **reciprocally** with changes in total peripheral vascular resistance:

- When total peripheral resistance is **normal** (100%), cardiac output is also normal.
- When total peripheral resistance **increases** above normal, cardiac output **falls**.
- When total peripheral resistance **decreases**, cardiac output **increases**.

This follows from Ohm's law (Chapter 14): **Cardiac output = Pressure / Resistance**

## DURING EXERCISE

Strenuous exercise is one of the most stressful conditions the normal circulatory system faces. Such a large mass of skeletal muscle requires large amounts of blood flow.

The cardiac output often must increase to:

- **Four to five times normal** in the nonathlete
- **Six to seven times normal** in the well-trained athlete

## SKELETAL MUSCLE BLOOD FLOW RATE

During rest, skeletal muscle blood flow averages **3 to 4 ml/min/100 g** of muscle.

During extreme exercise in the well-conditioned athlete, this blood flow can increase **25- to 50-fold**, rising to **100 to 200 ml/min/100 g** of muscle. Peak blood flows as high as **400 ml/min/100 g** have been reported for thigh muscles of endurance-trained athletes.

### Blood Flow During Muscle Contractions

Figure 21-1 shows blood flow changes in a calf muscle during strong rhythmic exercise:

- Flow increases and decreases with each muscle contraction.
- At the end of contractions, blood flow remains high for a few seconds, then returns to normal during the next few minutes.

The cause of lower flow during the muscle contraction phase is **compression of the blood vessels** by the contracted muscle. During strong tetanic contraction, blood flow can be almost stopped — but this also causes rapid weakening of the contraction.

### Increased Blood Flow in Muscle Capillaries During Exercise

During rest, some muscle capillaries have little or no flowing blood. During strenuous exercise, **all capillaries open**.

This opening of dormant capillaries:

- Diminishes the distance that oxygen and nutrients must diffuse
- Can contribute a **twofold to threefold** increased capillary surface area for diffusion

## CONTROL OF SKELETAL MUSCLE BLOOD FLOW

### Decreased Oxygen in Muscle Enhances Flow

The large increase in muscle blood flow during skeletal muscle activity is caused mainly by **chemicals released locally** that act on the muscle arterioles to cause dilation.

One of the most important chemical effects is **reduction of oxygen level** in muscle tissues. When muscles are active, they use oxygen rapidly, decreasing oxygen concentration in tissue fluids. This causes local arteriolar vasodilation.

**Adenosine** may be an important vasodilator substance. However, experiments show that even large amounts of adenosine infused directly into a muscle artery cannot increase blood flow to the same extent as during intense exercise. It also cannot sustain vasodilation in skeletal muscle for more than about 2 hours.

### Other Vasodilator Factors

Even after muscle blood vessels become insensitive to adenosine, other vasodilator factors continue to maintain increased capillary blood flow:

1. **Potassium ions**
2. **Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)**
3. **Lactic acid**
4. **Carbon dioxide**

### Nervous Control of Muscle Blood Flow

In addition to local tissue vasodilator mechanisms, skeletal muscles have:

- **Sympathetic vasoconstrictor nerves** (secrete norepinephrine)
- Sympathetic vasodilator nerves (in some species)

When maximally activated, sympathetic vasoconstriction can decrease blood flow through resting muscles to as little as **one-half to one-third normal**. This is physiologically important in attenuating decreases of arterial pressure in circulatory shock and during other periods of stress.

### Adrenal Medulla Contribution

The adrenal medullae secrete increased amounts of:

- **Norepinephrine** — acts on muscle vessels to cause vasoconstriction
- **Epinephrine** — often has a slight vasodilator effect (excites more beta-adrenergic receptors, which are vasodilator receptors)

## CIRCULATORY READJUSTMENTS DURING EXERCISE

Three major effects occur during exercise essential for the circulatory system to supply blood flow to the muscles:

1. **Sympathetic nervous system activation** in many tissues
2. **Increase in arterial pressure**
3. **Increase in cardiac output**

### Effects of Sympathetic Activation

At the onset of exercise, signals are transmitted from the brain:

- To the muscles to cause muscle contraction
- Into the vasomotor center to initiate sympathetic discharge

Simultaneously, parasympathetic signals to the heart are attenuated.

Three major circulatory effects result:

1. **Heart stimulation** — Heart rate and pumping strength increase due to sympathetic drive plus release from parasympathetic inhibition.

2. **Peripheral vasoconstriction/vasodilation** — Many arterioles are strongly contracted, **except** those in active muscles (which are vasodilated by local mechanisms). This "lends" blood supply to the muscles. Coronary and cerebral systems are **spared** this vasoconstrictor effect.

3. **Venous contraction** — Muscle walls of veins and other capacitative areas contract powerfully. This greatly increases the **mean systemic filling pressure** — one of the most important factors in promoting increased venous return and cardiac output.

### Sympathetic Stimulation Increases Arterial Pressure

An important effect of increased sympathetic stimulation in exercise is to **increase arterial pressure**. This results from:

1. Vasoconstriction of arterioles and small arteries in most tissues (except brain and active muscles)
2. Increased pumping activity by the heart
3. Great increase in mean systemic filling pressure caused mainly by venous contraction

These effects together increase arterial pressure during exercise by as little as **20 mm Hg** or as much as **80 mm Hg**, depending on conditions.
