## RESPIRATORY INSUFFICIANCE — PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The previous chapters discussed several methods for studying respiratory abnormalities, including:

- Vital capacity
- Tidal air
- Functional residual capacity
- Dead space
- Physiologic shunt
- Physiological dead space

This array of measurements is only part of the clinical pulmonary physiologist's tools.

## MAXIMUM EXPIRATORY FLOW

In many respiratory diseases, particularly asthma, resistance to airflow becomes especially great during expiration.

This sometimes causes tremendous difficulty in breathing.

### Concept of Maximum Expiratory Flow

When a person expires with great force, the expiratory airflow reaches a maximum flow.

This maximum flow cannot be increased even with greatly increased additional force.

The maximum expiratory flow is much greater when the lungs are filled with a large volume of air than when they are almost empty.

### Mechanism of Flow Limitation

Figure 43-1A shows the effect of increased pressure applied to the outsides of the alveoli and air passageways when the chest cage is compressed.

The arrows indicate that the same pressure compresses the outsides of the alveoli and bronchioles.

Therefore, this pressure:

- Forces air from the alveoli toward the bronchioles
- Tends to collapse the bronchioles at the same time

Once the bronchioles have almost completely collapsed, further expiratory force can still increase the alveolar pressure greatly.

However, it also increases the degree of bronchiolar collapse and airway resistance by an equal amount.

This prevents further increase in flow.

### Effect of Lung Volume on Maximum Expiratory Flow

Figure 43-1B shows the effect of different degrees of lung collapse (and therefore also of bronchiolar collapse) on the maximum expiratory flow.

The curve recorded shows the maximum expiratory flow at all levels of lung volume.

This occurs after a healthy person:

1. First inhales as much air as possible
2. Then expires with maximum expiratory effort until no greater rate can be achieved

Note that the person quickly reaches a maximum expiratory airflow of more than 400 L/min.

Regardless of how much additional expiratory effort the person exerts, this is still the maximum flow rate that can be achieved.

As the lung volume becomes smaller, the maximum expiratory flow rate also becomes less.

**Reason for this phenomenon:**

- In the enlarged lung, the bronchi and bronchioles are held open partially by elastic pull on their outsides by lung structural elements
- As the lung becomes smaller, these structures are relaxed
- The bronchi and bronchioles are collapsed more easily by external chest pressure
- This progressively reduces the maximum expiratory flow rate as lung volume decreases

## ABNORMALITIES OF THE MAXIMUM EXPIRATORY FLOW-VOLUME CURVE

Figure 43-2 shows the normal maximum expiratory flow-volume curve.

It also shows two additional flow-volume curves recorded in two types of lung diseases:

- Constricted lungs
- Partial airway obstruction

### Constricted Lungs

Constricted lungs have:

- Reduced total lung capacity (TLC)
- Reduced residual volume (RV)

Furthermore, because the lung cannot expand to a normal maximum volume, even with the greatest possible expiratory effort, the maximal expiratory flow cannot rise to equal that of the normal curve.

**Constricted lung diseases include:**

- Fibrotic diseases of the lung (tuberculosis, silicosis)
- Diseases that constrict the chest cage (kyphosis, scoliosis, fibrotic pleurisy)

### Airway Obstruction

In diseases with airway obstruction, it is usually much more difficult to expire than to inspire.

This is because the closing tendency of the airways is greatly increased by the extra positive pressure required in the chest to cause expiration.

By contrast, the extra negative pleural pressure that occurs during inspiration actually "pulls" the airways open at the same time.
