Endocrinology and Reproduction

# CHAPTER 81

## Male Sexual Physiology

### Function of the Testes

#### SPERMATOGENESIS

##### General Characteristics of Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis is the sequence of events by which spermatogonia develop into spermatozoa. It begins with the mitotic division of the spermatogonia in the seminiferous tubules. One of the spermatogonia divides to form two primary spermatocytes, each of which undergoes meiosis to form spermatids. The spermatids then undergo spermiogenesis (differentiation) to become spermatozoa.

##### Three Major Stages of Sperm Development

The process of sperm development involves three critical stages. First, the multiplication stage involves mitotic division of spermatogonia to maintain the germ cell line. Second, the growth stage involves primary spermatocytes increasing in size before meiosis. Third, the maturation stage involves meiosis, producing haploid spermatids that differentiate into spermatozoa.

##### Mature Sperm

Mature sperm are highly specialized cells composed of a head, middle piece, and tail. The head contains the condensed nucleus with DNA. The middle piece has mitochondria spirally arranged around the axoneme. The tail (flagellum) provides motility through whip-like movements.

### Male Reproductive Organs

The male reproductive system consists of several key organs. The **testes** are the primary sex organs that produce sperm and testosterone. The **epididymis** stores and matures sperm. The **vas deferens** transports sperm from the epididymis to the ejaculatory duct. The **seminal vesicles**, **prostate gland**, and **bulbourethral glands** produce seminal fluid. The **penis** is the organ of copulation.

---

### HORMONAL REGULATION OF MALE REPRODUCTIVE FUNCTION

#### Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal Axis

The male reproductive system is regulated by a complex hormonal cascade. The **hypothalamus** secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which stimulates the **anterior pituitary** to release luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). LH stimulates testosterone production by Leydig cells, while FSH promotes spermatogenesis by acting on Sertoli cells.

#### Testosterone and Its Functions

Testosterone is the primary androgenic hormone produced by the testes. It is responsible for:

- Development of male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics
- Maintenance of spermatogenesis
- Protein anabolic effects and muscle development
- Bone growth and mineral density
- Red blood cell production
- Behavioral effects including libido

#### Feedback Regulation

Testosterone exerts **negative feedback** on both the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, regulating LH and FSH secretion. Inhibin, produced by Sertoli cells, specifically inhibits FSH secretion. This feedback system maintains stable testosterone levels and proper spermatogenesis.

---

### SEMEN AND EJACULATION

#### Composition of Semen

Semen is composed of sperm from the testes and seminal fluid from accessory sex glands. The seminal vesicles contribute approximately 60% of the fluid volume, containing fructose, prostaglandins, and fibrinogen. The prostate gland contributes about 30%, providing citric acid, calcium, and proteolytic enzymes. The bulbourethral glands add a small amount of mucus.

#### Semen Quality

Normal semen parameters include:

- Volume: 1.5-5.0 mL
- Sperm concentration: ≥15 million/mL
- Total sperm count: ≥39 million per ejaculate
- Motility: ≥40% with progressive movement
- Morphology: ≥4% normal forms

#### Ejaculation Process

Ejaculation occurs in two phases. Emission involves contraction of the vas deferens and accessory glands, propelling sperm and seminal fluid into the prostatic urethra. Ejaculation proper involves rhythmic contractions of the bulbocavernosus and ischiocavernosus muscles, expelling semen through the urethra.

---

### Male Sexual Dysfunction

#### Erectile Dysfunction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is the inability to achieve or maintain penile erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance. Common causes include:

- **Vascular disease**: Impaired blood flow to the corpora cavernosa
- **Neurological disorders**: Damage to nerves involved in erection
- **Endocrine disorders**: Diabetes mellitus, hypogonadism
- **Medications**: Antidepressants, antihypertensives
- **Psychogenic factors**: Depression, anxiety, stress

#### Premature Ejaculation

Premature ejaculation is the most common male sexual dysfunction, characterized by ejaculation occurring with minimal sexual stimulation before, upon, or shortly after penetration. It may result from psychological factors or altered serotonin signaling.

#### Hypogonadism

Male hypogonadism is characterized by low testosterone production. It may be:

- **Primary (hypergonadotropic)**: Testicular failure with elevated LH/FSH
- **Secondary (hypogonadotropic)**: Pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction with low LH/FSH

---

### CRYPTORCHIDISM

Cryptorchidism (undescended testes) is one of the most common congenital anomalies in males. The testes normally descend from the abdomen into the scrotum during fetal development. If descent does not occur, infertility risk increases, particularly if bilateral. Treatment options include:

- **Hormonal therapy**: hCG or GnRH to stimulate testicular descent
- **Surgical correction (orchiopexy)**: relocating the testes to the scrotum, ideally before 1-2 years of age

---

### MALE CONTRACEPTION

Current male contraceptive methods are limited to:

- **Condoms**: Barrier method preventing sperm entry
- **Vasectomy**: Surgical occlusion of the vas deferens

Research continues on hormonal male contraceptives using testosterone with progestins or GnRH antagonists to suppress spermatogenesis reversibly.

---

### AGING AND MALE REPRODUCTIVE FUNCTION

#### Andropause

Testosterone levels decline gradually with aging (approximately 1% per year after age 30). Some men develop symptoms similar to female menopause, including:

- Decreased libido
- Fatigue and depression
- Loss of muscle mass and bone density
- Increased body fat

#### Prostate Changes

The prostate gland enlarges with age (benign prostatic hyperplasia), potentially causing urinary symptoms. Prostate cancer risk increases with age.

---

### CLINICAL CORRELATIONS

#### Klinefelter Syndrome

47,XXY karyotype causes male phenotype with small testes, tall stature, gynecomastia, and infertility due to azoospermia.

#### Turner Syndrome

45,X karyotype results in female phenotype with streak gonads, short stature, and lack of puberty without hormone replacement.

#### Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome

Complete androgen insensitivity results in female external genitalia but XY karyotype and intra-abdominal testes due to nonfunctional androgen receptors.

---

### SUMMARY

Male reproductive function involves:

1. Spermatogenesis in the seminiferous tubules, requiring FSH and high intratesticular testosterone
2. Testosterone production by Leydig cells, stimulated by LH
3. Maturation and storage of sperm in the epididymis
4. Addition of seminal fluid by accessory sex glands
5. Ejaculation mediated by sympathetic nervous system
6. Erection achieved through parasympathetic-mediated vasodilation
7. Complex hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal feedback regulation
8. Decline in function with aging but potential for reproduction well into later life

## Female Physiology Before Pregnancy and Female Hormones

### FEMALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

#### Overview

The female reproductive system is designed to produce ova, receive sperm, support fertilization, and sustain fetal development. The system includes the ovaries (producing ova and hormones), fallopian tubes (transporting ova and site of fertilization), uterus (implantation and fetal development), and vagina (birth canal and receptacle for sperm).

#### Hormonal Control

The female reproductive system operates under complex hormonal control involving:

- **Hypothalamus**: Secretes GnRH in a pulsatile manner
- **Anterior pituitary**: Releases FSH and LH in response to GnRH
- **Ovaries**: Produce estrogen, progesterone, inhibin, and androgens
- **Feedback loops**: Both negative and positive feedback regulate hormone secretion

---

### THE OVARIAN CYCLE

#### Phases of the Ovarian Cycle

The ovarian cycle consists of three phases:

1. **Follicular phase (Days 1-13)**: Follicle development under FSH influence
2. **Ovulation (Day 14)**: LH surge triggers release of mature oocyte
3. **Luteal phase (Days 15-28)**: Corpus luteum forms and produces progesterone

#### Folliculogenesis

Follicle development progresses through stages:

- Primordial follicle → Primary follicle → Secondary follicle → Tertiary (antral) follicle → Preovulatory follicle → Corpus luteum

Each month, multiple follicles begin development, but typically only one reaches ovulation (dominant follicle selection).

#### Ovulation

Ovulation is triggered by a dramatic LH surge (approximately 10-fold increase). The preovulatory follicle ruptures, releasing the secondary oocyte into the peritoneal cavity. Ovum pickup occurs by the fimbriated end of the fallopian tube.

#### Corpus Luteum

After ovulation, the granulosa and theca cell layers luteinize to form the corpus luteum. This structure produces progesterone and estrogen during the luteal phase. If pregnancy does not occur, the corpus luteum regresses (luteolysis) at the end of the cycle.

---

### THE UTERINE (MENSTRUAL) CYCLE

#### Endometrial Changes

The uterine cycle coordinates with the ovarian cycle:

1. **Menstrual phase (Days 1-4)**: Endometrial shedding due to corpus luteum regression and withdrawal of progesterone
2. **Proliferative phase (Days 5-14)**: Estrogen-driven endometrial regeneration and growth
3. **Secretory phase (Days 15-28)**: Progesterone-induced endometrial maturation and glandular secretion in preparation for implantation

#### Menstruation

Menstruation involves:

- Spiral artery vasoconstriction
- Endometrial ischemia and necrosis
- Sloughing of functional layer
- Blood and tissue loss (average 30-40 mL)

---

### OVARIAN HORMONES

#### Estrogens

Estrogen types include:

- **Estradiol (E2)**: Primary estrogen during reproductive years
- **Estrone (E1)**: Predominant after menopause
- **Estriol (E3)**: Major estrogen during pregnancy

**Functions of Estrogen:**

- Development of female secondary sexual characteristics
- Growth and maintenance of reproductive organs
- Endometrial proliferation
- Bone growth and maintenance
- Cardiovascular protection
- Effects on CNS (mood, cognition)

#### Progesterone

**Functions of Progesterone:**

- Transformation of endometrium to secretory phase
- Thickening of cervical mucus (inhibits sperm passage)
- Decreased uterine contractility
- Breast development for lactation
- Thermogenic effect (increases basal body temperature)
- Anxiolytic effects

#### Inhibin

Inhibin is produced by granulosa cells and specifically inhibits FSH secretion, providing negative feedback.

---

### CONTROL OF THE MENSTRUAL CYCLE

#### Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Ovarian Axis

The menstrual cycle is controlled by intricate feedback mechanisms:

- **Early follicular phase**: Low estrogen and progesterone → increased GnRH/FSH/LH
- **Mid-cycle**: Rising estrogen → positive feedback → LH/FSH surge
- **Luteal phase**: High progesterone and estrogen → negative feedback → suppressed GnRH/FSH/LH

#### The LH Surge

The LH surge results from estrogen's positive feedback effect on the pituitary, occurring when estradiol levels exceed approximately 200 pg/mL for 36-48 hours. This surge triggers ovulation and initiates luteinization.

#### Feedback Summary

| Phase | GnRH | FSH | LH | Estrogen | Progesterone |
|-------|------|-----|-----|----------|--------------|
| Early Follicular | ↑ | ↑ | Normal | Low | Low |
| Late Follicular | ↑ | ↓ (neg feedback) | ↑ (pos feedback) | ↑↑ | Low |
| Ovulation | ↑ | ↑ (FSH surge) | ↑↑ (LH surge) | Peak | Low |
| Luteal | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | ↑ | ↑↑ |

---

### FERTILIZATION AND EARLY DEVELOPMENT

#### Fertilization

Fertilization typically occurs in the ampulla of the fallopian tube:

1. Sperm capacitation in female reproductive tract (5-6 hours)
2. Sperm binding to zona pellucida (ZP3 glycoprotein)
3. Acrosome reaction releasing enzymes
4. Sperm penetration of zona pellucida
5. Fusion of sperm and oocyte membranes
6. Cortical reaction (blocks polyspermy)
7. Second meiotic division completion
8. Pronucleus formation and syngamy

#### Cleavage and Implantation

Following fertilization:

- Cleavage divisions produce blastomeres
- Morula forms (Days 3-4)
- Blastocyst forms (Day 5)
- Hatching from zona pellucida
- Implantation in endometrium (Days 6-10)

---

### CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS

#### Common Menstrual Disorders

- **Dysmenorrhea**: Painful menstruation, often due to prostaglandins
- **Menorrhagia**: Heavy menstrual bleeding
- **Amenorrhea**: Absence of menstruation
- **Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)**: Cyclical symptoms occurring after ovulation

#### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

PCOS is characterized by:

- Hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne)
- Chronic anovulation (oligomenorrhea, amenorrhea)
- Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound
- Insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction

Treatment includes lifestyle modification, hormonal contraceptives, and ovulation induction agents for fertility desires.

#### Menopause

Menopause (cessation of menstruation for 12 months) results from ovarian follicular depletion. Associated changes include:

- Estrogen deficiency symptoms (hot flashes, vaginal atrophy)
- Increased cardiovascular risk
- Bone loss (osteoporosis risk)
- Mood changes

---

### SUMMARY

Female reproductive physiology involves:

1. Coordinated hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian hormone regulation
2. Cyclical ovarian follicle development and ovulation
3. Cyclical endometrial changes preparing for potential pregnancy
4. Positive and negative feedback mechanisms governing the menstrual cycle
5. Successful fertilization requiring capacitated sperm and mature oocyte
6. Hormonal support (hCG from pregnancy) maintaining corpus luteum function
7. Transition to menopause as ovarian function declines
