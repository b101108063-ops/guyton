## CARBON DIOXIDE DISSOCIATION CURVE

The CO2 dissociation curve (Figure 41-14) depicts the dependence of total blood CO2 in all its forms on PCO2.

The normal blood PCO2 is within a narrow range:
- **Arterial blood:** 40 mm Hg
- **Venous blood:** 45 mm Hg

The normal concentration of CO2 in the blood in all its different forms is about 50 volume percent.

During normal transport of CO2 from tissues to lungs, only 4 volume percent is exchanged:
- Concentration rises to ~52 volume percent as blood passes through tissues
- Concentration falls to ~48 volume percent as blood passes through lungs

## WHEN OXYGEN BINDS WITH HEMOGLOBIN, CO2 IS RELEASED (THE HALDANE EFFECT)

An increase in CO2 in the blood causes O2 to be displaced from hemoglobin (the Bohr effect), which is an important factor in increasing O2 transport.

The reverse is also true: binding of O2 with hemoglobin tends to displace CO2 from the blood.

This effect is called the **Haldane effect**, and it is quantitatively far more important in promoting CO2 transport than the Bohr effect is in promoting O2 transport.

### Mechanism of the Haldane Effect

The Haldane effect results from the simple fact that the combination of O2 with hemoglobin in the lungs causes the hemoglobin to become a stronger acid.

This displaces CO2 from the blood into the alveoli in two ways:

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Carbaminohemoglobin displacement** | The more highly acidic hemoglobin has less tendency to combine with CO2, displacing CO2 from the carbamino form |
| **Bicarbonate conversion** | Increased acidity causes hemoglobin to release excess H+, which binds with HCO3− to form carbonic acid, dissociating into water and CO2 |

The CO2 is then released from the blood into the alveoli and finally into the air.

### Quantitative Significance of the Haldane Effect

Figure 41-15 demonstrates the significance of the Haldane effect on CO2 transport from tissues to lungs.

The figure shows two CO2 dissociation curves:
1. **PO2 = 100 mm Hg** — in the blood capillaries of the lungs
2. **PO2 = 40 mm Hg** — in the tissue capillaries

**At point A:** The normal PCO2 of 45 mm Hg in the tissues causes 52 volume percent of CO2 to combine with the blood.

**On entering the lungs:**
- PCO2 falls to 40 mm Hg
- PO2 rises to 100 mm Hg

**Without the Haldane effect:** CO2 content would fall only to 50 volume percent (loss of only 2 volume percent).

**With the Haldane effect:** The increase in PO2 lowers the CO2 dissociation curve, so CO2 content falls to 48 volume percent (point B).

**Conclusion:** The Haldane effect approximately doubles the amount of CO2 released from blood in the lungs and doubles the amount of CO2 picked up in the tissues.

## CHANGE IN BLOOD ACIDITY DURING CO2 TRANSPORT

The carbonic acid formed when CO2 enters the blood in peripheral tissues decreases blood pH.

Reaction of this acid with the acid-base buffers of the blood prevents H+ concentration from rising very much (and pH from falling very much).

### Normal pH Changes

| Condition | pH Value |
|-----------|----------|
| Arterial blood | ~7.41 |
| Venous blood (after tissue capillaries) | ~7.37 |

The pH change during normal conditions is only 0.04 unit.

When CO2 is released from blood in the lungs, pH rises back to 7.41.

### Heavy Exercise or Sluggish Blood Flow

During heavy exercise or other conditions of high metabolic activity, or when blood flow through the tissues is sluggish:
- The decrease in pH in tissue blood can be as much as 0.50
- This is about 12 times normal
- This causes significant tissue acidosis

## RESPIRATORY EXCHANGE RATIO

Normal transport from lungs to tissues by each 100 ml of blood:
- **O2 transported:** ~5 ml
- **CO2 transported:** ~4 ml

Under normal resting conditions, only about 82% as much CO2 is expired from the lungs as O2 is taken up by the lungs.

### Definition

The ratio of CO2 output to O2 uptake is called the **respiratory exchange ratio (R)**:

```
R = Rate of carbon dioxide output / Rate of oxygen uptake
```

### R Values Under Different Metabolic Conditions

| Metabolic Condition | R Value |
|---------------------|---------|
| Carbohydrates exclusively | 1.00 |
| Fats exclusively | 0.70 |
| Normal mixed diet | 0.825 |

### Explanation of R Value Differences

When O2 is metabolized with carbohydrates, one molecule of CO2 is formed for each molecule of O2 consumed.

When O2 reacts with fats, a large share of the O2 combines with hydrogen atoms from the fats to form water instead of CO2.

Thus, when fats are metabolized, the respiratory quotient of the tissue chemical reactions is about 0.70 instead of 1.00.

For a person on a normal diet consuming average amounts of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins, the average value for R is **0.825**.

## RESPIRATORY CENTER

The nervous system normally adjusts the rate of alveolar ventilation to meet the demands of the body almost exactly.

The result is that oxygen partial pressure (PO2) and carbon dioxide partial pressure (PCO2) in arterial blood are hardly altered, even during heavy exercise and most other types of respiratory stress.

### Components of the Respiratory Center

The respiratory center is composed of several groups of neurons located bilaterally in the **medulla oblongata** and **pons** of the brain stem.

It is divided into three major collections of neurons:

| Collection | Location | Primary Function |
|------------|----------|------------------|
| **Dorsal respiratory group** | Dorsal portion of the medulla | Mainly causes inspiration |
| **Ventral respiratory group** | Ventrolateral part of the medulla | Mainly causes expiration |
| **Pneumotaxic center** | Dorsally in the superior portion of the pons | Controls rate and depth of breathing |

## DORSAL RESPIRATORY GROUP OF NEURONS — CONTROLS INSPIRATION AND RESPIRATORY RHYTHM

The dorsal respiratory group of neurons plays a fundamental role in the control of respiration.

It extends most of the length of the medulla.

Most of its neurons are located in the **nucleus of the tractus solitarius (NTS)**, although additional neurons in the adjacent reticular substance of the medulla also play important roles.

The NTS is the sensory termination of both the vagal and the glossopharyngeal nerves, which transmit sensory signals into the respiratory center from:

- Peripheral chemoreceptors
- Baroreceptors
- Receptors in the liver, pancreas, and multiple parts of the gastrointestinal tract
- Several types of receptors in the lungs

### Rhythmical Inspiratory Discharges

The basic rhythm of respiration is generated mainly in the dorsal respiratory group of neurons.

Even when all peripheral nerves entering the medulla have been sectioned, and the brain stem has been transected above and below the medulla, this group of neurons still emits repetitive bursts of inspiratory neuronal action potentials.

The basic cause of these repetitive discharges is unknown.

In primitive animals, neural networks have been found in which:
1. Activity of one set of neurons excites a second set
2. The second set inhibits the first
3. After a period of time, the mechanism repeats itself

Similar networks of neurons are present in humans, located entirely within the medulla.

These networks probably involve not only the dorsal respiratory group but adjacent areas of the medulla as well, and are responsible for the basic rhythm of respiration.

### Inspiratory "Ramp" Signal

The nervous signal transmitted to the inspiratory muscles (mainly the diaphragm) is not an instantaneous burst of action potentials.

Instead, it has the following characteristics:
- Begins weakly
- Increases steadily in a ramp manner for ~2 seconds in normal respiration
- Then ceases abruptly for ~3 seconds

The cessation turns off diaphragm excitation and allows elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall to cause expiration.

Then the cycle repeats.

**Advantage of the ramp signal:** It causes a steady increase in lung volume during inspiration, rather than inspiratory gasps.

### Two Controllable Qualities of the Inspiratory Ramp

| Quality | Control Mechanism | Effect |
|---------|-------------------|--------|
| **Rate of increase** | Controls how rapidly the ramp rises | During heavy respiration, the ramp increases rapidly, filling the lungs rapidly |
| **Limiting point** | Controls when the ramp ceases | Earlier cessation → shorter inspiration → shorter expiration → increased respiratory frequency |

## PNEUMOTAXIC CENTER — LIMITS DURATION OF INSPIRATION AND INCREASES RESPIRATORY RATE

The pneumotaxic center is located dorsally in the nucleus parabrachialis of the upper pons.

It transmits signals to the inspiratory area.

**Primary effect:** Controls the "switch-off" point of the inspiratory ramp, thereby controlling the duration of the filling phase of the lung cycle.

### Effect of Signal Strength

| Signal Strength | Inspiration Duration | Lung Fill |
|-----------------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Strong** | As little as 0.5 second | Slight filling |
| **Weak** | 5 or more seconds | Much greater filling |

### Function of the Pneumotaxic Center

The function of the pneumotaxic center is primarily to limit inspiration.

This has a secondary effect of increasing the rate of breathing because limitation of inspiration also shortens expiration and the entire period of each respiration.

- **Strong pneumotaxic signal:** Can increase rate to 30–40 breaths/min
- **Weak pneumotaxic signal:** May reduce rate to only 3–5 breaths/min

## VENTRAL RESPIRATORY GROUP OF NEURONS — FUNCTIONS IN BOTH INSPIRATION AND EXPIRATION

Located in each side of the medulla, about 5 millimeters anterior and lateral to the dorsal respiratory group of neurons, is the ventral respiratory group of neurons.

It is found in:
- The nucleus ambiguus (rostrally)
- The nucleus retroambiguus (caudally)

### Differences from Dorsal Respiratory Group

| Function | Dorsal Group | Ventral Group |
|----------|--------------|---------------|
| **Normal quiet respiration** | Active; causes inspiration via diaphragm | Almost totally inactive |
| **Expiration in quiet breathing** | Results from elastic recoil | No active participation |
| **Basic rhythmical oscillation** | Primary driver | Does not participate |
| **During increased ventilation demand** | Base oscillation | Receives spill-over signals; contributes extra drive |
| **Response to stimulation** | Causes inspiration | Some cause inspiration, others cause expiration |

### Key Points

1. **Normal quiet breathing** is caused only by repetitive inspiratory signals from the dorsal respiratory group transmitted mainly to the diaphragm. Expiration results from elastic recoil.

2. The ventral respiratory neurons do not appear to participate in the basic rhythmical oscillation that controls respiration.

3. When the respiratory drive for increased pulmonary ventilation becomes greater than normal, respiratory signals spill over into the ventral respiratory neurons from the basic oscillating mechanism of the dorsal respiratory area.

4. Electrical stimulation of some neurons in the ventral group causes inspiration; stimulation of others causes expiration.

5. The ventral group provides powerful expiratory signals to the abdominal muscles during very heavy expiration. Thus, this area operates as an overdrive mechanism when high levels of pulmonary ventilation are required, especially during heavy exercise.

## LUNG INFLATION SIGNALS LIMIT INSPIRATION — THE HERING-BREUER INFLATION REFLEX

In addition to central nervous system respiratory control mechanisms operating within the brain stem, sensory nerve signals from the lungs also help control respiration.

### Stretch Receptors

Located in the muscular portions of the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles throughout the lungs are stretch receptors.

When the lungs become overstretched, these receptors transmit signals through the vagi into the dorsal respiratory group of neurons.

### Effect on Inspiration

These signals affect inspiration similarly to signals from the pneumotaxic center.

When the lungs become overinflated, the stretch receptors activate a feedback response that "switches off" the inspiratory ramp and stops further inspiration.

This mechanism is called the **Hering-Breuer inflation reflex**.

This reflex also increases the rate of respiration, as is true for signals from the pneumotaxic center.

### Activation Threshold in Humans

In humans, the Hering-Breuer reflex is probably not activated until the tidal volume increases to more than three times normal (>1.5 L/breath).

Therefore, this reflex appears to be mainly a protective mechanism for preventing excess lung inflation rather than an important factor in normal control of ventilation.

## CONTROL OF OVERALL RESPIRATORY CENTER ACTIVITY

Up to this point, the basic mechanisms for causing inspiration and expiration have been discussed.

It is also important to know how the intensity of the respiratory control signals is increased or decreased to match the ventilatory needs of the body.

For example, during heavy exercise, the rates of O2 usage and CO2 production increase dramatically.

The respiratory center must adjust accordingly to maintain proper blood gas levels.

### Factors Affecting Respiratory Center Activity

- Metabolic rate of tissues
- Level of physical activity
- Blood pH and CO2 levels
- Oxygen levels in arterial blood
